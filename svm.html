Inline Question 1:¶

It is possible that once in a while a dimension in the gradcheck will not match exactly. What could such a discrepancy be caused by? Is it a reason for concern? What is a simple example in one dimension where a gradient check could fail? How would change the margin affect of the frequency of this happening? Hint: the SVM loss function is not strictly speaking differentiable

Answer: With scores $s_j = W_j \cdot x_i$, the hinge loss is

$$L_i = \sum_{j \ne y_i} \max(0,\ s_j - s_{y_i} + 1)$$

and its gradient uses the indicator $\mathbb{1}(J) = 1$ if class $j$ violates the margin ($s_j - s_{y_i} + 1 > 0$ and $j \ne y_i$), $0$ otherwise:

$$\frac{\partial L_i}{\partial W_j} = \mathbb{1}(J)\, x_i, \qquad \frac{\partial L_i}{\partial W_{y_i}} = -\sum_{j \ne y_i} \mathbb{1}(J)\, x_i$$

Cause of the mismatch: $\max(0,\cdot)$ has a kink where $s_j - s_{y_i} + 1 = 0$, so the loss is not differentiable there and $\mathbb{1}(J)$ jumps from 0 to 1. The numerical gradient uses a centered difference $\frac{L(W+h) - L(W-h)}{2h}$. If a margin is within $h$ of the kink, the two evaluation points fall on opposite sides, so the numerical estimate averages the two slopes while the analytic gradient picks one side.

Is it a concern? No. It is an artifact of checking the gradient numerically at a non-differentiable point, not a bug in the analytic gradient. It affects only an occasional dimension, and gradient descent works fine with this subgradient.

1-D example: $f(x) = \max(0, x)$ at $x = 0$. The analytic gradient we use is 0 (with the indicator convention $\mathbb{1} = 0$ when the margin is not $> 0$), but the numerical gradient is $\frac{f(h) - f(-h)}{2h} = \frac{h - 0}{2h} = 0.5$.

Effect of the margin: Call the margin $\Delta$ (it is $1$ in the lecture formula). At initialization $W$ is small, so all $s \approx 0$ and every term is $s_j - s_{y_i} + \Delta \approx \Delta = 1$, far from the kink, so failures are rare. Making $\Delta$ smaller (toward 0) puts many terms right at the kink and makes mismatches much more frequent; a larger $\Delta$ makes them rarer. In general the frequency depends on how many margin terms land within $h$ of 0.

Inline question 2:¶

Describe what your visualized SVM weights look like, and offer a brief explanation for why they look they way that they do.

Answer: The weights look like blurry, grainy templates of each class, dominated by color. The clearest ones: car has a red body with a blue area on top (windshield/sky), frog is a bright green blob in the center, deer has a brown center on a green background, horse has a dark and pinkish shape in the middle above a yellow-brown bottom, truck has a red/purple lower half, and plane and ship are bluish overall, with ship darker blue at the bottom (water). Bird, cat and dog are fainter reddish-brown or greenish blobs.

Why: A linear classifier $f(x, W) = Wx$ scores class $j$ as $s_j = W_j \cdot x_i$, so each $W_j$ acts as a template matched against the image. Training pushes $W_j$ toward the pixel patterns common in class $j$, mostly overall color and background layout, which is why color blobs dominate. Each class gets only one template, so all poses, colors and left/right orientations in a class are averaged together, which makes the templates blurry. They stay grainy because the best model used the weakest regularization I tried ($\lambda$ = 1e4, with lr = 2e-7) and only 1500 SGD iterations: weak L2 regularization $\lambda R(W) = \lambda \sum w^2$ does little to suppress weights on individual noisy pixels. Stronger regularization smooths the templates but lowered validation accuracy in my sweep (0.376 at $\lambda$ = 2.5e4 and 0.363 at $\lambda$ = 5e4, vs 0.392 at 1e4, for lr = 2e-7)
